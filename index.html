The Still field guide · Focus, sound, and recovery

Build a calmer, more deliberate workday.

A timer is useful when it helps you make a clear decision: what deserves your attention now, when to pause, and how to return. This guide explains the ideas behind timed work and ambient sound, then turns them into a practical routine you can adapt to your own energy and responsibilities.

01 / Attention

The Science of Deep Focus: Why the Pomodoro Technique Rewires Productivity

The familiar Pomodoro pattern is straightforward: choose a task, work for about 25 minutes, take a short break, and repeat. Its value is less about a magic number than about making the start of work small enough to attempt. “Write the report” is vague and intimidating; “draft the first two paragraphs before this timer ends” names a visible next action. A defined end point also gives you permission to defer unrelated requests until the interval is over.

Attention is selective. While you read, calculate, or compose, the mind must keep the current goal active and resist competing information. A notification, nearby conversation, or remembered errand can pull that goal out of the foreground. Returning to the original task takes time because you must reconstruct what you were doing and what comes next. A focus interval creates a temporary boundary: capture the interruption in a note, then resume the same small objective.

What 25 minutes can and cannot claim

The 25-minute block is a convenient starting point, not a proven universal attention span. People, tasks, sleep, stress, and the surrounding environment change how long sustained work feels comfortable. Some projects benefit from a longer uninterrupted period; others require much shorter starts. Still uses 25 minutes because it is easy to plan around, while the stopwatch is available when an open-ended session makes more sense.

Ultradian rhythms describe biological patterns that recur more often than once a day, including changes associated with sleep and alertness. They are sometimes used to argue that everyone should work for exactly 90 minutes or exactly 25 minutes. That conclusion goes further than the evidence. A biological rhythm does not prescribe a single ideal timer length for writing, coding, studying, or caregiving. Observe when your concentration actually fades and adjust your blocks accordingly.

Why the break belongs in the plan

Continuous effort can increase fatigue and make a task feel harder to restart. Planned pauses give you a chance to move, hydrate, rest your eyes, and notice whether your original goal is still the right one. Research on breaks and attention is mixed across tasks and settings, but there is evidence that thoughtfully placed breaks can support engagement. A classroom study of micro-breaks reported more consistent performance in its break condition; that finding does not establish an exact schedule for every workplace.

Use the five-minute break after a completed focus block as a change of state, not merely a chance to scroll through a different stream of demands. Stand, stretch gently, look into the distance, or take a short walk if possible. After several blocks, the 15-minute long break offers enough room for food, fresh air, or a fuller reset. If your body needs a pause sooner, take one. A timer should support good judgment, not override it.

“Rewires productivity” is best understood as a metaphor for practicing a repeatable behavior: define a task, begin, recover, and return. A single session does not rewire a brain in a measurable way, and this tool is not a treatment for attention or mental-health conditions. The practical gain is a dependable routine that reduces the number of decisions required to begin the next useful step.

02 / Sound

Acoustic Psychology: How Ambient Rain, Fire, and White Noise Block Distractions

Sound can either carry information or form a relatively steady background. Understandable speech often competes with verbal work because your attention may follow words even when you are trying to ignore them. A gentle, predictable soundscape may make an intermittent hallway voice or sudden household noise less prominent. This is called masking: the background sound reduces the contrast between a distraction and the rest of the acoustic environment. It does not remove the original sound or guarantee concentration.

In one small laboratory study, masking background speech with nature sound reduced a short-term-memory disruption caused by that speech. Another study of speech and task performance examined how speech intelligibility and masking conditions affected cognitive work. These results help explain why some people prefer neutral sound, while leaving plenty of room for personal preference, task type, and volume to change the outcome.

Choose a texture that fits the task

Rain is usually the softest option here: a filtered wash that can soften sharp changes in the room. Campfire has lower, irregular texture and can feel less uniform, which some listeners find comforting and others find distracting. White noise spreads energy across a broad range of frequencies and may cover hiss or speech edges more strongly, but it can become tiring at high volume. None of these sounds is inherently superior. Try each briefly during the kind of work you actually do.

Still generates its soundscapes in the browser with Web Audio. The rain and fire buttons shape locally generated noise with filters; the white-noise button uses a broader noise texture. You can combine sounds, change the volume, or turn them all off. No prerecorded sound file is required for the sound engine. Your browser may need a click before it allows audio to play, and sound may stop or pause if your device sleeps.

Protect the work and your hearing

Set volume only high enough to soften interruptions, especially when wearing headphones. If you must raise it to cover loud speech, consider moving, closing a door, or using comfortable hearing protection when appropriate. A soundscape is a modest environmental adjustment, not a substitute for solving an unsafe or chronically noisy workspace. Silence is also a valid setting: some reading, language learning, and music tasks work better without any added audio.

Watch for the novelty trap. Switching among sounds every few minutes can itself become an interruption. Pick one texture before starting the timer and leave it alone for the block. At the break, ask a practical question: did this background help me return to the task after a disturbance, or did I keep attending to the sound? Your own answer is more useful than assuming that a named frequency or sound color must improve performance.

03 / Practice

4-Step Daily Focus Protocol: From Intention to Active Recovery

Step 01

Name one outcome

Before pressing Start, write a result you could recognize at the end of the block: “identify three sources,” “test the login path,” or “draft the opening paragraph.” If the task is large, choose its first concrete action. Keep a separate capture list for ideas or obligations that surface while working. This reduces the pressure to handle every thought immediately.

Step 02

Prepare the environment

Close tabs that do not serve the chosen outcome, silence avoidable notifications, and put needed materials within reach. Select rain, fire, white noise, or quiet at a comfortable level. Then start a 25-minute Pomodoro. If your schedule only permits ten minutes, use the timer as a planning cue rather than treating the full block as a requirement.

Step 03

Work and record interruptions

When another task appears, jot it down in a few words and return to the visible next step. If the interruption is urgent, deal with it; the timer can be paused and resumed. When you reach a stopping point, leave a breadcrumb such as “next: compare the two estimates.” That sentence makes the next session easier to start than an empty page or a half-remembered problem.

Step 04

Recover and review

Take the five-minute break after a block, or a 15-minute break after several. Move away from the screen when you can. Before the next block, check what was completed, what changed, and whether the next intention is still realistic. The completed-session count measures blocks finished during this visit; it is a prompt to reflect, not a score of your worth or the quality of your work.

A daily plan can be as light as two blocks for your most important task, a longer break, and one block for maintenance. Reserve flexibility for meetings, fatigue, and unexpected demands. If a task requires sustained creative flow, switch to the stopwatch rather than interrupting a productive stretch just to satisfy a preset interval. If a task feels impossible to begin, lower the threshold: spend one block gathering materials or outlining questions. Useful work is often the act of making the next step possible.

At the end of the day, review the capture list and decide what deserves a place tomorrow. Keep the routine humane. Breaks, sleep, collaboration, and off-screen responsibilities are part of a productive life, even when they do not appear in a session counter. The best protocol is the one that helps you start, finish, and recover repeatedly without turning every minute into a performance metric.

04 / Answers

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is a 25-minute Pomodoro scientifically optimal for everyone?

No. Twenty-five minutes is a practical convention, not a universal biological limit. Try it as a starting interval, then adjust to the task and your energy. A shorter block can help with a difficult start; a longer stretch can suit uninterrupted writing or problem solving. The important pattern is an intentional goal followed by a genuine pause when you need one.

How should I use short and long breaks?

After one focus block, choose the five-minute short break for movement, water, or a visual rest. After roughly four blocks, try the 15-minute long break for a fuller reset. You do not have to wait for a timer to address discomfort, hunger, or another real need. Breaks work best when they change your activity rather than simply replacing work with another demanding screen.

Do rain, campfire, or white noise improve concentration?

They may help some people by masking intermittent sound, especially intelligible speech, but effects vary with the person, room, task, and listening level. Start quietly and compare one block with sound to one in silence. If the texture itself catches your attention or leaves you tired, lower the volume or turn it off. A calmer room and fewer notifications may help more than any soundscape.

Does Still save my sessions or require an account?

No account is required. The current timer, completed-session count, selected sounds, and volume are held in this page's memory during your visit. Reloading or closing the page resets them. The sound engine creates audio locally in your browser. Separate third-party requests, including fonts and the AdSense script, are explained in the Privacy Policy.

What happens if I leave the tab or my device sleeps?

The timer uses your device clock to estimate elapsed time, so a background tab can usually catch up when you return. Browser throttling, sleep, clock changes, and audio restrictions can affect the display or completion sound. Keep the page open when you want a chime, and use a dedicated alarm for time-critical commitments. The stopwatch counts upward when you prefer an open-ended work session.
